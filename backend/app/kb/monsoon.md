# Reading the monsoon & drought signal

## Monsoon basics
The southwest monsoon delivers ~75% of India's annual rain between June and September.
"Onset" is declared over Kerala around 1 June and covers most farms by mid-July. Monsoon
arrives in bursts (active spells) separated by dry "breaks"; a 2-3 week break in July-August
is normal and not, by itself, a drought.

## What a 30-day rainfall anomaly means
Comparing the last 30 days with the 1991-2020 average for the same dates removes season
effects. Values within ±20% are normal variability. Below -20% the topsoil starts drying for
unirrigated crops; below -40% is an agricultural drought watch — prioritise irrigation for
flowering/grain-filling stages and delay new sowing of rain-fed crops.

## Consecutive dry days
Counting days with under 1 mm of rain measures soil recharge gaps. In the monsoon season,
7-10 dry days stress shallow-rooted crops; in winter (rabi), 3 rain-free weeks is normal and
irrigation scheduling matters more than rain watching.

## Reading rain probability
A 60% probability does not mean 60% of the day rains — it means that in 6 of 10 similar
atmospheric setups, measurable rain (≥0.1 mm) occurred at that spot. For decisions, combine
probability with expected amount: 60% × 15 mm is actionable; 60% × 1 mm is a drizzle risk.

## Evapotranspiration (ET₀)
ET₀ estimates how much water a reference crop loses to the air per day (FAO-56 method).
When weekly rain < weekly ET₀, the difference is the irrigation your crop needs. Hot, dry,
windy weather raises ET₀ sharply; cloudy humid weather lowers it.

## Soil moisture layers
Satellite-era reanalysis tracks moisture at several depths. The 0-1 cm layer reacts within
hours (look for germination conditions); 3-9 cm reflects the seed zone; 27-81 cm is the deep
reserve tapped mid-season. Rising shallow moisture with a falling deep layer means recent
rain hasn't penetrated — roots remain at risk.
